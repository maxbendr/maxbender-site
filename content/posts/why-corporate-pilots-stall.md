Every corporate startup program I have run has the same shape. Month one is enthusiasm. Month two is scoping. Month three is a pilot that technically works. Month four is silence.

The silence is the interesting part. By then the startup has shipped, the business unit has seen the numbers, and nobody has said no. The pilot simply stops being anyone's job.

## The pilot was never attached to a budget

A pilot that comes out of an innovation budget is a science project. Nobody in the business unit has to defend it, which sounds comfortable until the moment you need them to fight for it.

The fix is unglamorous. Before the pilot starts, find the line item the rollout would come from and the person who owns it. If that person cannot name the number, you do not have a pilot, you have a demo.

## Nobody agreed what success looks like

Most pilot kickoffs end with a vague sense that the thing should "prove value." Four months later two people remember two different definitions and both of them are disappointed.

Write down one metric, one threshold, and one date. Put it in the kickoff deck where the sponsor can see it. Ugly and specific beats elegant and open ended.

## The startup optimized for the pilot instead of the rollout

Founders are good at making a pilot succeed. They will staff it with their best engineer and answer Slack messages at midnight. That is exactly what you want and exactly what hides the real question, which is whether the thing survives procurement, security review, and a support model that does not depend on the founder.

Ask early: what does this look like when you are not personally in the room. If the answer is uncomfortable, better to hear it in week two.

## What actually helps

Three things, in order of how often I see them ignored.

1. A named budget owner in the room at kickoff, not a delegate.
2. One success metric written down before anyone touches a system.
3. A rollout conversation in month two, while the pilot is still going well.

None of that is clever. It is just the difference between a program that produces case studies and a program that produces contracts.
